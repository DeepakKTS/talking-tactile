Textbook figures a blind student can touch, and that talk back.

A teacher turns a printed figure into a raised sheet and sets a phone above it. The student explores with one finger. Rest on a part and the phone says what it is. Ask a question out loud and the answer comes from that same figure and the teacher’s notes.

A college science textbook can have a picture on every other page. Very few of those pictures are ever made into something a blind student can touch.

Mostly it comes down to time, because each one is made by hand.

How it works

The teacher prepares a sheet once. After that, the student can come back to it as often as they like.

Making the sheet

What the teacher does, on a computer

  1. Upload a figureA PNG, a JPEG or a PDF page. Muse Spark reads it, lists the parts in a few words each, and plans each one for touch: an area, a line or a group.
  2. Each part gets an outlineSAM 3.1 outlines every part from points Spark placed on it, with no training on that figure. Code, not a model, lays out the sheet in millimeters: gaps, textures, point symbols and braille.
  3. Check every partFix an outline with two clicks, merge, rename, describe. A part becomes touchable only once the teacher checks it.
  4. Print itPublish, print at 100 percent, and raise the lines on swell paper or with puffy paint. The sheet comes with a map of where every part sits.

Reading the sheet

What the student does, with a phone and no screen

  1. Set the sheet under a phoneA sighted helper taps the four printed corner marks once. The camera finds the fingertip, and the corners map it to the page.
  2. Touch a partA texture tone on entering, then its name when the finger rests. Touch needs no internet once the sheet has loaded from the Mac.
  3. Ask about itHold the screen and talk. Muse Voice Transcribe listens, with the figure’s part names as hints. Muse Spark answers from the figure or the teacher’s notes, or sends the student to the teacher.

Built on muse-spark-1.3, sam-3.1 and muse-voice-transcribe-1.0 through the Meta Model API. No model redraws the figure: a student learns from the textbook’s own picture.

The choices behind it

Four corner marks, nothing more

The sheet carries the figure, its braille and four printed corner marks. A sighted helper taps the marks once on the phone. No tags or stickers go on the paper.

The teacher has the last word

Models make mistakes with diagrams. A part is touchable only once a teacher has checked it, and publishing refuses a sheet that breaks a tactile rule.

Rules come from published guidance

I have no background in tactile graphics, so every spacing, texture and braille value comes from published guidance (BANA, APH, tactilegraphics.org, the NPS and the ADA), with its source next to it in config/tactile_rules.yaml: at least 3.2 mm between raised parts, at most five textures, point symbols of at least 6.4 mm.

Answers stay on the figure

An answer comes from the figure’s parts and labels or the teacher’s notes, under a strict schema. A question the figure does not answer gets one fixed sentence that sends the student to the teacher.

Geometry comes from code

Directions, neighbors and distances are computed in code. The language model never guesses them.

A phone first

An ordinary phone camera is enough today. A path for the camera in Meta’s glasses is on the list for after the hackathon.

Not done yet

  • Not yet tested with blind students or teachers. No tester session or expert review happened before the deadline.
  • The phone numbers are not measured yet: fingertip error on the printed test sheet, touch to tone, and question latency on the iPhone (eval/gate-b/README.md).
  • Voice is tested on synthetic speech only, in English (eval/qa/VOICE.md).
  • Questions need the network and are slow: 5.0 s at p50 on a Mac, against a 2.0 s budget in RULES.md.
  • A sighted helper sets the corners, and nothing detects the phone or the sheet moving afterward.
  • Small parts are hard: 6 of the flower’s 13 areas need the teacher (eval/plan/RESULTS.md).
